3. Key equations

Photon energy
E = hν = hc/λ;   c = λν
Energy per photon (J). Shorter λ → higher E. For per-mole, multiply by NA.
Coulomb's law
E ∝ q1q2/r
Bigger charges and smaller ions → stronger attraction → larger lattice energy, higher melting point.
Ideal gas law
PV = nRT
T in kelvin; with R = 0.08206 use atm and L.
Boyle's law
P1V1 = P2V2
Constant n and T.
Dalton's law
PA = XAPtotal = nART/V
Each gas behaves as if alone in the container.
Gas density
d = Pℳ/RT
Heavier molar mass → denser gas at the same P and T.
Henry's law
S = kHPgas
Gas solubility is proportional to its partial pressure above the liquid.
Molarity
M = mol solute / L solution;   mmol = mL × M
Mass percent
mass % = (mass of part / mass of whole) × 100
Bond energies
ΔH ≈ Σ BDE(bonds broken) − Σ BDE(bonds formed)
Breaking bonds costs energy (+); forming bonds releases energy (−).
Enthalpies of formation
ΔH°rxn = Σ nΔH°f(products) − Σ nΔH°f(reactants)
Elements in standard states (O2(g), Fe(s), …) have ΔH°f = 0. Watch the phase: H2O(l) ≠ H2O(g).
Calorimetry
qsoln = mcΔT;   ΔHrxn = −qsoln/n
Temperature drops → endothermic (ΔH > 0). Temperature rises → exothermic (ΔH < 0).
Gibbs free energy
ΔG = ΔH − TΔS
Spontaneous when ΔG < 0.

4. Step-by-step procedures

4.1 Precipitation stoichiometry (limiting reagent)

  1. Write the precipitate's formula from the ion charges (e.g., Mg2+ + PO43− → Mg3(PO4)2).
  2. Write the net ionic equation with coefficients (3 Mg2+ + 2 PO43− → Mg3(PO4)2).
  3. mmol of each ion = mL × M (× ions per formula unit of the salt).
  4. Divide each ion's mmol by its coefficient; the smaller result is the limiting ion and equals mmol of precipitate.
  5. Grams = mol precipitate × molar mass.

4.2 Concentration of an ion left after precipitation

  1. Do steps 1–4 above.
  2. mmol left = mmol at start − (coefficient × mmol precipitate).
  3. Divide by the TOTAL mixed volume (V1 + V2), not one solution's volume.
  4. Spectator ions (Na+, K+, NO3−, Cl− here) are only diluted.

4.3 ΔH from bond dissociation energies

  1. Draw full Lewis structures for every reactant and product.
  2. List every bond broken (reactants) and formed (products), with counts and multiplicity (C=O vs C–O).
  3. Cancel bonds that appear unchanged on both sides (optional, but reduces arithmetic).
  4. ΔH = Σ broken − Σ formed; check the sign (most combustions are strongly negative).

4.4 ΔH°rxn from ΔH°f

  1. Balance the equation.
  2. Multiply each ΔH°f by its coefficient (elements = 0).
  3. Products minus reactants.
  4. For a net ionic reaction use the ions' ΔH°f values and only the species that actually react.

4.5 Coffee-cup calorimetry

  1. Mass of solution = water + dissolved solid (unless told otherwise).
  2. qsoln = m c ΔT with ΔT = Tfinal − Tinitial (sign matters).
  3. qprocess = −qsoln; convert J → kJ.
  4. Divide by moles of the substance asked about.

4.6 Mass percent and density → molarity

  1. Assume 100 g of solution.
  2. g solute = mass %; mol solute = g ÷ molar mass.
  3. Volume of solution = 100 g ÷ density (g/mL) → convert mL to L.
  4. M = mol ÷ L.

4.7 VSEPR geometry

  1. Count valence electrons (add for negative charge, subtract for positive).
  2. Place the least electronegative atom in the center; complete octets on outer atoms; leftover electrons go on the central atom (expanded octet allowed for period 3+).
  3. Electron domains = bonded atoms + lone pairs on the central atom (a double/triple bond counts once).
  4. Electron geometry from domains (2 linear, 3 trigonal planar, 4 tetrahedral, 5 trigonal bipyramidal, 6 octahedral); molecular shape from atoms only.
  5. Hybridization: 2 → sp, 3 → sp2, 4 → sp3, 5 → sp3d, 6 → sp3d2.

4.8 Assigning oxidation states

  1. Free elements = 0; monatomic ions = their charge.
  2. F = −1; O = −2 (except peroxides, −1); H = +1 with nonmetals (−1 in metal hydrides).
  3. Sum = overall charge; solve for the unknown.
  4. Oxidized = number goes up (loses e−) → it is the reducing agent. Reduced = number goes down → it is the oxidizing agent.

4.9 Net ionic equations

  1. Check solubility rules: Group 1, NH4+, NO3−, CH3COO− salts are soluble; most Cl−/Br−/I− soluble except Ag+, Pb2+, Hg22+; most SO42− soluble except Ba2+, Pb2+, Sr2+, Ca2+ (slightly); most CO32−, PO43−, OH−, S2− insoluble unless with Group 1/NH4+.
  2. Split strong electrolytes (soluble salts, strong acids, strong bases) into ions.
  3. Keep solids, liquids (H2O), gases, and weak electrolytes together.
  4. Cancel spectator ions; check atom and charge balance.

4.10 Finding chiral centers

  1. Look only at sp3 carbons (no double bonds, no CH2 or CH3).
  2. A chiral center has four DIFFERENT groups — compare whole groups, not just the first atom.
  3. A molecule with exactly one chiral center is chiral; enantiomers are non-superimposable mirror images.

4.11 Curved-arrow mechanisms

  1. Arrows start at electrons (lone pair or bond) and point to where they go.
  2. Nucleophile = electron-rich (lone pair, π bond, negative charge); electrophile = electron-poor (δ+ carbon, e.g., C=O carbon).
  3. Check formal charges and octets after each step; a carbonyl attack goes through a tetrahedral intermediate.

4.12 Electron configurations of transition-metal ions

  1. Write the neutral atom ([Ar] 4s2 3dn; Cr and Cu are 4s1).
  2. Remove 4s electrons FIRST, then 3d.
  3. Fill d orbitals by Hund's rule (one in each of the 5 before pairing) and count unpaired electrons.

5. Common mistakes

  1. Forgetting to convert nm → m (× 10−9) in E = hc/λ, or °C → K in gas laws.
  2. Flipping the sign in bond-energy problems: it is broken − formed (reactants − products), the reverse of the ΔH°f formula.
  3. Using H2O(g) data when the equation has H2O(l) (they differ by 44 kJ/mol).
  4. Calorimetry sign: a temperature DROP means the process absorbed heat (ΔH > 0).
  5. Using only one solution's volume after mixing; concentrations after mixing use V1 + V2.
  6. Ignoring the precipitate's coefficients when finding the limiting ion (divide mmol by 3 for Mg2+ in Mg3(PO4)2).
  7. Removing 3d electrons before 4s when forming transition-metal cations.
  8. Counting a double bond as two electron domains in VSEPR.
  9. Thinking breaking bonds releases energy — breaking bonds ALWAYS costs energy; only bond formation releases it.
  10. Confusing molecular shape with electron geometry (H2O: tetrahedral electron geometry, bent shape).
  11. Calling a symmetric molecule with polar bonds (CO2, CCl4, BF3) polar.
  12. Thinking a CH2 or a C=C carbon can be a chiral center.
  13. Mixing up oxidizing and reducing agents: the oxidizing agent is the one REDUCED.
  14. Rounding too early — keep extra digits until the end, then report the sig figs of the least precise data.

6. Units and significant figures

EnergyJ, kJ (1 kJ = 1000 J); per photon in J; per mole in kJ/mol
Pressureatm, torr, mmHg, kPa
VolumeL, mL (1 L = 1000 mL = 1000 cm3)
ConcentrationM = mol/L; mM = mmol/L (1 M = 1000 mM)
TemperatureK for every gas law and ΔG = ΔH − TΔS; ΔT in °C equals ΔT in K
Specific heatJ·g−1·K−1
Enthalpy changekJ (for the reaction as written) or kJ/mol (per mole of a named substance)
  • Multiplying/dividing: keep the fewest significant figures of the data.
  • Adding/subtracting: keep the fewest decimal places (this is why ΔH°f sums keep one decimal place).
  • Exact numbers (coefficients, counted atoms, 1000 mL/L) don't limit sig figs.
  • On a multiple-choice exam, sig figs rarely decide the answer — but unit and sign errors often do.

7. Study plan for the week before the final

  1. Take one timed mock final (110 min) cold to find weak topics.
  2. Each day: one topic-practice set (20 questions) on your two weakest topics, plus the Adaptive review.
  3. Re-work every worked solution you missed on paper without looking.
  4. Two days before: a second mock final. Night before: re-read sections 3–5 only.

Note: the questions in the practice set are original, written to match the exams' topics and style; they are not copied from the exams. Structure drawings and graphs from the exams could not be read from the PDFs, so structure-based skills are practiced in words and formulas.
